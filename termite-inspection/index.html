What to expect from a termite inspection in Rockhampton

In short: A termite inspection is a visual check of the accessible parts of a property, outside, subfloor, inside and roof space, usually reported against AS 4349.3 or AS 3660.2. It can't guarantee a house is termite-free, so regular inspections matter.

How to prepare, what the inspector looks at, and what the report will and won't tell you.

Last updated: 24 October 2026

The two Australian Standards you'll hear about

AS 4349.3-2010, Inspection of buildings, Part 3: Timber pest inspections, is the standard for timber pest inspections, including the pre-purchase inspections buyers order. The pest industry association AEPMA advises asking for an inspection to AS 4349.3 when you're buying, and says it covers termites, wood borers and fungal decay.

AS 3660.2:2017, Termite management, Part 2: In and around existing buildings and structures, covers termite management for buildings that are already standing, including inspections and treatment of existing homes.

Ask the inspector which standard their report follows.

Before the inspector arrives

  • Clear access to the roof space hatch, subfloor, meter box, garage and storerooms.
  • Move stored items away from walls where you can, and unlock sheds and gates.
  • Tell the inspector about any past termite activity, treatment or barrier paperwork.
  • Note any leaks, damp areas or soft timber you've noticed.

AEPMA's code of practice says the inspector needs a roof access hatch of at least 45 cm by 40 cm to enter a roof void, and reasonable clearance under a raised floor. Areas that can't be safely reached are noted as not inspected.

What happens on the day

AEPMA's code describes an inspection that usually starts outside, noting the lie of the land and any extensions, then moves through the subfloor, the interior and the roof void. The inspector checks accessible timber visually and may tap (sound) and probe suspect timber, and uses a moisture meter to find damp areas. Thermal cameras and movement detectors are optional extra tools that some inspectors use.

What the report covers, and its limits

Expect a written report noting any live termites, past damage, other timber pests and decay, the areas inspected and not inspected, and conducive conditions: things that make attack more likely, such as leaks, gardens or soil against walls, firewood or timber touching the ground, poor subfloor ventilation and high ground levels.

A standard inspection is visual and non-invasive, so it can't guarantee a property is free of termites, particularly behind walls or under floor coverings. AEPMA's code says a standard pre-purchase inspection doesn't cover drywood termites unless extra work is agreed in advance.

Buying a home

In Queensland, termite inspection reports and advice are covered by QBCC termite management licences, so ask whoever does a pre-purchase timber pest inspection for their QBCC licence number. Book it early, because the time to finish inspections under a contract can be short.

How often to inspect

The QBCC recommends a termite inspection at least every 12 months, and more often in high-risk areas. CSIRO also recommends inspections at least once a year.
